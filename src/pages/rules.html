About this prototype

How the letter checks work

The letter checker reads an evidence letter, works out what type it is, and runs 42 checks taken from the Legal Aid Agency's evidence guidance. It then gives a confidence rating that the letter meets the requirements.

It is a screening aid. The Legal Aid Agency makes every decision.

1. Working out the letter type

Each type of evidence has different requirements, so the checker first looks for wording that identifies the type. It tries the types in this order and uses the first match:

  1. Refuge refusal – mentions a refuge and a refusal, or someone seeking admission to a refuge
  2. Refuge stay – mentions a refuge and someone being admitted or staying there
  3. Support organisation – mentions six months of operation, where the organisation is situated, or "the matters I have relied upon"
  4. MARAC – mentions a MARAC, a risk panel or a safeguarding forum
  5. Health professional – says the injuries or condition are consistent with domestic abuse, or that the person was examined
  6. Social services – the letter describes itself as from social services, a social worker or children's services
  7. Financial abuse – mentions financial abuse, a bank, a loan or a credit card
  8. Public authority – mentions a family relationship, an assessment, or "domestic abuse by" someone
  9. IDVA or ISVA – mentions an IDVA or ISVA, or someone providing support

Support organisation letters are recognised before MARAC letters because they often mention a MARAC referral as one of the reasons for their judgement.

If none of these match, the checker says it does not recognise the letter and the person can choose the type themselves. Police records, court orders and Home Office letters are not checked.

2. Running the checks

Every check gives one of three results:

OK
The letter has what the guidance asks for.
Change needed
The letter is missing something the guidance requires, or says it in a way the guidance does not accept.
Check
Something worth checking that would not on its own stop the letter being accepted, such as the author's role not being stated.

When a check finds a problem, it also says how sure it is:

How sureMeaningExample
CertainThe letter contains wording that the guidance does not accept."might be consistent with domestic abuse", "we provided support"
ProbablyRequired wording was not found. It may be there in words the checker does not know.No date of the refuge refusal
PossiblyA judgement call that a person should look at.The matters relied on look too general

3. The confidence rating

The rating starts at 100% and goes down for each problem found:

FindingRating is multiplied by
Change needed – certain0.05
Change needed – probably0.25
Change needed – possibly0.6
Check0.9
Letter type with no real examples to test against (MARAC, refuge stay, social services, financial abuse)0.9
RatingLevelResult shown
80% or moreHighLooks ready if no check needs a change
40% to 79%MediumCheck – ask your solicitor
Under 40%LowNeeds changes

For example, a refuge letter with no perpetrator named and no family relationship gets 100% × 0.25 × 0.25 = 6% (low). A support organisation letter whose matters look too general gets 100% × 0.6 = 60% (medium).

! WarningThe rating is not a measured probability. The numbers were chosen by hand from the guidance, not fitted to Legal Aid Agency decisions. A rating of 60% shows the checker is less sure than at 90%, not that 6 in 10 such letters are accepted.

How well it works

The checks work on letters that use the wording in the GOV.UK sample letters. They do not yet work on letters written in the author's own words.
Tested againstCorrect
12 example letters from the hackathon evidence pack12 of 12
46 edited versions of the example letters, and 8 fictional letters54 of 54
404 fictional letters from our own templates, kept back while the checks were tuned402 of 404
13 fictional GP and support service letters in varied wording, from a separate review pack0 of 13

Here "correct" means the checker recognised the letter type, gave a high rating to a letter that meets the requirements, and a medium or low rating to one that does not.

The review pack shows the main weakness. Its letters say the same things in different words – "consistent with the effects of domestic abuse", "has operated continuously since 2014" – and the checks look for the sample-letter wording. All 8 complete letters were rated low or not recognised, and 4 of the 5 incomplete letters were not recognised.

What these numbers do not show

  • Our fictional letters come from the same templates as the letters used for tuning, so they are much easier than real letters.
  • The review pack's labels were proposed by an AI model, not checked by caseworkers. 3 of its 16 letters are marked as needing human review and are not counted.
  • None of these numbers measure agreement with Legal Aid Agency caseworkers. That needs a sample of real, anonymised letters with caseworker decisions.
